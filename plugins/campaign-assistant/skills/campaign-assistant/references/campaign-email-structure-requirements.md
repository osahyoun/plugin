# Campaign Email Structure Requirements

Source reviewed: campaign email writing guide PDF

This reference summarizes the petition-email structure taught in the campaigner training deck. Treat it as a mandatory drafting structure for petition mailings, especially when deciding what belongs above the first petition link, below it, in the box text, and in the source section.

## Hard Requirement For Petition Drafts

Every petition email draft must follow this structure unless the user explicitly asks for a different format:

1. Box text
2. Above the Link (ATL), with 2-4 short paragraphs
3. First petition landing-page link / CTA immediately after ATL
4. Below the Link (BTL), with supporting evidence and context
5. Second petition landing-page link / CTA in BTL or near the close
6. Movement story
7. More information / sources

The two petition links must point to the same action and use the same core ask. This keeps the member action singular while giving both fast-deciding and skeptical readers a clear path to the petition landing page.

## Required Email Parts

A typical campaign email should include:

1. Box text
2. Above the Link (ATL), also called the lede
3. First petition link / call to action
4. Below the Link (BTL)
5. Second petition link / call to action
6. More information / sources

## Above the Link (ATL)

ATL is the short opening section before the first petition link. It should persuade a supporter to act quickly.

Requirements:

- Keep ATL short: 2-4 short paragraphs maximum.
- Assume most readers are on mobile, so paragraphs should be brief and easy to scan.
- Include urgency, momentum, and theory of change.
- Write as if explaining to a close friend why they should take action now.
- Make the opening sentence earn attention immediately.
- Do not overload ATL with background detail. Put deeper evidence and context below the link.

An effective ATL should contain:

- A strong opening sentence.
- A crisistunity: the live crisis or opportunity where action can change what happens next.
- A theory of change: why this action, aimed at this target, can plausibly move the outcome.

## Opening Sentence

The opening sentence should answer: "Why should I read on?"

Requirements:

- Lead with a concrete development, stake, or tension.
- Prefer a live fact, vivid consequence, or strategic opportunity over static background.
- Avoid starting with a dry institutional description unless it immediately creates urgency.
- Make the reader feel the issue is current and actionable.

Good opening patterns:

- Breaking news or a fresh development.
- A concrete harm with a named actor.
- A surprising opportunity to stop or win something.
- A direct statement that links the issue to the target.

Weak opening patterns:

- Broad issue summaries with no immediate action path.
- Generic statements that could fit any campaign.
- Background facts that do not yet show why the reader should act.

## Crisitunity

Crisitunity is the part of the email that shows the reader there is a crisis or opportunity and that acting now can change the course of events.

Requirements:

- Name the real-world situation.
- Make clear whether the moment is a crisis to prevent or an opportunity to win.
- Show how the reader can intervene.
- Explain the cause -> effect sequence the campaign is trying to create.
- Keep it concrete and current.

Good crisistunity logic:

- If enough people pressure the target now, the target can change course.
- If the target changes course, a specific harm can be prevented or a specific win can be achieved.
- The moment is time-sensitive or strategically open.

Avoid:

- Generic urgency with no decision point.
- Big-picture harm without a practical intervention.
- "Tell X to do Y" without explaining why X can act or why now matters.

## Theory of Change

Theory of change explains how the campaign can win using the leverage available.

Requirements:

- State the "if X, then Y" logic clearly.
- Name the target or decision-maker.
- Explain why public pressure, reputational pressure, political pressure, consumer pressure, or member action can influence that target.
- Make the action feel plausible, not guaranteed.
- Connect the supporter action to the campaign aim.

Useful theory-of-change shapes:

- If enough supporters sign now, the target will see public pressure before a decision.
- If key governments oppose a proposal, the proposal can be blocked.
- If a company faces reputational pressure, it may cut ties with a harmful supplier or practice.
- If a public figure, regulator, or institution is already under pressure, member action can increase the cost of inaction.

Avoid:

- Claiming a petition guarantees a win.
- Leaving the pressure mechanism implicit.
- Naming a target without explaining why that target matters.

## First Petition Link / CTA

The first petition link comes after ATL, once the case for immediate action is clear.

Requirements:

- Keep the CTA concise, short, and direct.
- Use one main verb.
- Name the target where possible.
- Name the demand in plain language.
- Send the reader to the petition landing page.
- Avoid long explanatory CTA text.
- If the petition URL is not known, include a clear `[PETITION LINK]` placeholder in the draft rather than omitting the link position.

Recommended pattern:

```text
Tell [target]: [specific demand].
```

Examples of CTA shape:

- Tell Walmart: protect your customers -- stop selling assault weapons.
- Tell Nestle to come clean and cut ties with palm oil supplier REPSA.
- Tell Chevron to take responsibility and pay what it owes to affected communities.

## Box Text

Box text is the short summary that appears around the action box or petition module. Its job is to drive action with the fewest words possible.

Requirements:

- Summarize the email simply.
- Keep it short.
- Make the first 6 words especially strong because they may appear in preview contexts.
- Make the action clear without repeating the whole email.
- Focus on the target, demand, or most urgent stake.

Box text should answer:

- What is happening?
- Who needs to act?
- What should the reader do?

## Below the Link (BTL)

BTL expands and supports the argument after the first petition link. It is for readers who need more detail before acting.

Requirements:

- Add supportive information that did not fit in ATL.
- Include facts for more discerning readers.
- Provide background and context for readers who are not following the issue.
- Include stories of people affected by the issue, especially marginalized voices, where sourced and appropriate.
- Include the timeframe or deadline, while also making sure the deadline appears above the link.
- Develop the protagonist and antagonist clearly.
- Break up walls of text with the repeated petition call to action or link.
- Include the movement story.

BTL should answer:

- What would it take for a skeptical person to take action?
- What additional evidence supports the ask?
- Who is harmed, who has power, and how can supporters intervene?

## Movement Story

The movement story connects this email to the broader work of the organization and the supporter community.

Requirements:

- Show the bigger picture.
- Connect the issue to past or similar campaign work.
- Use social proof when similar actions have worked before.
- Explain how this issue fits into a larger problem or system.
- For corporate campaigns, explain how the issue affects corporate power more broadly.
- Keep the movement story tied to action, not organizational self-praise.

Useful movement-story material:

- Similar past campaign wins.
- Growing public pressure.
- Partner or coalition activity.
- The role of members in increasing pressure.
- The broader system the campaign is challenging.

## More Information / Sources

The email should include reputable supporting sources.

Requirements:

- Include 2 sources as a typical baseline.
- More sources are fine when needed, but keep them reputable.
- Use sources to support factual claims, not as decoration.
- Prefer sources that establish the problem, target responsibility, decision window, and theory of change.

## Formatting And Readability

Requirements:

- Make the email scannable.
- Use bolding to highlight key bits of information so readers can extract the argument quickly.
- Avoid dense walls of text.
- Keep language informal but anti-oppressive.
- Treat readers as humans, not just action-takers.
- Make sure the email can stand alone.
- Show, do not just tell.
- Keep member focus clear, while being careful with "us" and "them" framing.
- For corporations, use "it" rather than "they" because corporations are not people.

## Petition Draft Checklist

Before a petition email is ready, check:

- Does the ATL have 2-4 short paragraphs?
- Does the opening sentence make the reader want to continue?
- Is there a clear crisistunity?
- Is there a clear theory of change?
- Is the first petition link placed after the ATL?
- Is the CTA short, concrete, and target-specific?
- Does the box text summarize the action in very few words?
- Does the BTL provide enough evidence for skeptical readers?
- Does the email include affected people, context, deadline, and target dynamics where relevant?
- Does the BTL include a second petition landing-page link or CTA?
- Do both petition links point to the same action and reinforce the same core ask?
- Does the email include a movement story?
- Are the sources reputable and sufficient?
- Is the copy scannable on mobile?
- Does the email stand alone without relying on unstated context?
