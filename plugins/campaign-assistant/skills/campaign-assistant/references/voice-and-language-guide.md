# Voice And Language Guide

This is the campaign email voice and progressive language reference for the Campaign Assistant plugin. It condenses the drafter voice and language guidance:

- internal golden style guide
- progressive language guidelines

## Golden Style Guide

Use this as the primary style authority when it conflicts with any single historical example.

### Non-Negotiables

- Lead with one concrete development, stake, or supporter-facing consequence.
- Keep paragraphs short enough to scan on mobile.
- Use direct second-person language, but do not sound theatrical or self-congratulatory.
- Keep one clear ask, one main verb, and one decision-maker whenever possible.
- Make urgency feel earned by the evidence, not inflated by rhetoric.

### Checklist

- Open fast and clearly. Do not bury the point.
- Move from stake to action without long scene-setting.
- Name what the supporter can do today.
- Keep the CTA practical and singular.
- End with a warm collective signoff and use PS only when it adds pressure or clarity.

### Prefer

- right now
- this week
- can you
- take two minutes
- here is what we know

### Avoid

- history will judge us
- everyone knows
- there is no choice
- shocking beyond belief
- we can no longer stay silent

### Sentence And Paragraph Style

- Mostly short-to-medium sentences.
- Use one sharper sentence before the CTA if it helps focus attention.
- Avoid dense blocks of exposition.

### Rhetorical Patterns

- Open on a concrete trigger, then explain the stake for ordinary people.
- Use practical framing before abstract moral flourish.
- Repeat the ask once with slightly different wording if it helps clarity.

### Structure And Flow

- Hook with a real development or tightly grounded supporter stake.
- Explain why this matters now in one compact turn.
- For non-petition emails, ask for one action near the end, not scattered throughout.
- For petition emails, follow the required petition structure: ATL first, one petition CTA immediately after ATL, BTL evidence/context, then repeat the same petition CTA in BTL or near the close. This is one ask in two placements, not two separate asks.
- Use PS as a final pressure line, not as a second essay.

### Action Orientation

- The action should feel quick, specific, and worth doing today.
- When the decision-maker is known, name them.
- Do not imply that the action guarantees the outcome.

### Anti-Patterns

- Copying distinctive wording from historical emails.
- Turning frustration into unsupported claims of refusal or bad faith.
- Generic activist filler that could fit any campaign.
- Over-claiming facts the evidence does not support.

## Progressive Language Guidelines

Use these checks when drafting or reviewing campaign copy.

### Core Principles

- Center people and shared humanity. Avoid reducing people to a condition, legal status, economic status, age, disability, race, gender, housing status, immigration status, incarceration history, or experience of violence.
- Use the names and terms that people and communities use for themselves. If a preferred term is unknown, choose the most specific respectful term available and flag the uncertainty.
- Use active voice when naming harm. Make the relevant actor, institution, company, state body, or system visible instead of hiding agency in passive constructions.
- Use proper nouns and clear referents. Do not let repeated "it", "this", or "that" blur who is acting, who is harmed, or who can change the outcome.
- Use precise, issue-specific language. Distinguish legal terms, advocacy terms, medical terms, and community terms.
- Avoid identity terms as insults or metaphors. Do not use age, disability, race, gender, immigration, poverty, housing, incarceration, or violence language casually to describe unrelated situations.
- Assume complexity. Do not flatten people into a single identity, stereotype, vulnerability, heroic narrative, or victim narrative.
- Do not repeat stigma even when the source does. Police reports, institutional statements, press releases, and common media phrases may carry bias.

### Issue-Area Guidance

Age:

- Usually omit age unless relevant.
- Prefer "older person", "young person", "teenager", or the person's chosen term.
- Avoid "the elderly", "the old", "senile", and ageist metaphors.

Disability:

- Prefer people-first or identity-first wording according to the person or community involved.
- Avoid framing disability only as a medical problem.
- Avoid "handicapped", "wheelchair-bound", "confined to a wheelchair", "retarded", "crazy", "lame", "victim of", and "suffering from".

Economy and class:

- Be specific about material circumstances.
- Prefer "people experiencing poverty", "low-income households", "people experiencing homelessness", "racial equity", and "living wage" where accurate.
- Avoid "the poor", "the needy", "less fortunate", "lazy", "unskilled labor", and class-coded judgments.

Environment and science:

- Use terms precisely and connect harms to direct impacts on lives, livelihoods, and communities.
- Avoid caricatures such as "tree hugger" or "greenie".
- Treat terms like "climate refugee" with care.

Food:

- Foreground food sovereignty, access, worker welfare, and local stories.
- Use program names accurately.
- Do not use famine, hunger, or malnutrition casually.

Gender and sex:

- Use people's chosen names and pronouns.
- Use "they" when a pronoun is unknown.
- Avoid gendered job titles and assumptions about heterosexuality or binary sex/gender.
- Avoid "homosexual", "transgendered", "sex change", "sexual preference", "man up", "mankind", and terms that reduce sex workers to their work.

Geopolitics:

- Write from the perspective of local people and global community, not empire or hierarchy.
- Prefer accurate place names and terms such as "Global South" and "Global North" where useful.
- Avoid "Third World", "Second World", "war on terror", and condescending phrases.

Health:

- Frame health as a human right and center access, dignity, and lived experience.
- Avoid "AIDS victim", "full-blown AIDS", and "suffering from AIDS".
- Use reproductive-health terms carefully and avoid imported opposition frames when a more accurate rights-based term is available.

Housing and space:

- Use people-first language and frame housing as a human right.
- Avoid criminalizing or fear-based terms such as "bum", "vagrant", "transient", or "the homeless".
- Prefer "homeless people", "people experiencing homelessness", or the affected person's chosen wording.

Immigration and refugees:

- Avoid us-versus-them framing, preserve individual stories, presume innocence, and never describe a person as illegal.
- Prefer "undocumented immigrant", "asylum seeker", "refugee", "stateless person", "person seeking citizenship", or "person with citizenship in..." where accurate.
- Avoid "illegal immigrant", "illegal alien", "alien", "anchor baby", and "failed asylum seeker".

Indigeneity and ancestry:

- Center Indigenous land, sovereignty, and self-determination.
- Use specific nation, tribe, community, or people names where known.
- Capitalize proper names.
- Avoid colonial possession language and outdated or caste-like terms unless quoting or legally necessary.

Police and incarceration:

- Separate the act from the person and use decriminalizing language.
- Prefer "incarcerated person", "person in prison", "person with conviction", "formerly incarcerated person", or chosen terms.
- Avoid "offender", "ex-offender", "felon", "the incarcerated", and language that equates public safety with criminalization.

Race and ethnicity:

- Tell stories from the perspective of the community represented.
- Be specific and use preferred terms.
- Avoid unnecessary racial identifiers, but name whiteness when race is relevant.
- Be careful with "minority", "person of color", and continental labels.
- Avoid "Caucasian", "Oriental", "colorblind", "thug", "ghetto" as an adjective, and "you people".

Sexual and domestic violence:

- Use consent-centered language, avoid victim-blaming, and do not report through the abuser's lens.
- Rape or assault is not sex; abuse is not an affair.
- Use "survivor" unless the person chooses "victim".
- Use content warnings for explicit descriptions.
- Avoid "accuser", "child porn", and language that makes gender-based violence only a women's issue.

### Drafting And Review Checks

- Does the copy name the relevant actor or institution responsible for harm?
- Does it avoid defining people by a condition, legal status, poverty, housing status, incarceration history, or trauma?
- Does it use the most specific community-preferred names available in the brief or source evidence?
- Does it avoid imported institutional or opposition frames when a rights-based or community-centered term is more accurate?
- Does it avoid casual metaphors that draw on disability, age, race, gender, immigration, poverty, incarceration, or violence?
- Does it preserve dignity without sentimentalizing, heroizing, pitying, or flattening affected people?
- If the draft uses any uncertain identity or community term, does it flag the uncertainty for campaigner review?
